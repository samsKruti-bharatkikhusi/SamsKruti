SamsKruti · Design brief · For handoff

From identity to system

SamsKruti has a visual identity. It does not have a design system.

Written for the designer taking this on. Sections 01–03 are the argument; 04–09 are the work. Every number below was measured against the repository, not estimated.

The diagnosis. Nineteen tokens exist and are bypassed 473 times — a ratio of about 25 to 1. The tokens are decorative; the real values live inline. The top route prefixes (seg- 139, hub- 92, side- 60, hr- 58) confirm it: styling is organised by page, so nothing is reusable and every new screen starts from zero.

The deeper problem is what colour means. Today colour encodes editorial identity — jade is the streets segment, red is food, indigo is crafts. It does not encode function. There is no colour that means “this is the primary action” or “this is destructive.” A designer cannot answer “what colour is the button” without first asking “which page is it on.” That is an expression system, and it is why the product cannot be designed consistently.

Not a criticism of the taste. The identity is genuinely good and much of it deserves to survive. What is missing is the layer underneath it: parts, states, and rules.

02 · The user

One person, and only him

Every decision resolves against Aklavya: a first-time visitor to Varanasi with no prior knowledge of the city, its streets, or its customs. He is not a culture enthusiast browsing at leisure. He is anxious, and he wants to be told what to do next.

He works bottom-up through his needs. He cannot feel anything about the ghats at dawn while he is still unsure whether the man following him is a threat, what a boat should cost, or whether his shoes are about to cause offence. Utility first, then emotion. A design that opens with atmosphere and defers logistics has the order backwards.

He also does not speak the product’s internal language. He does not know what Galiyan, Swaad or Hriday mean, and asking him to choose between them asks him to already understand the city he arrived confused about.

Consequence: the system must be designed light-first. The harder surface is a phone screen at midday, and the current dark-only palette cannot do that job at all. Night becomes the secondary mode, not the default — the reverse of how it is built now.

04 · Principles

Five rules to design against

  1. Colour encodes function, not location

    One calm identity colour and one action colour. A hot colour appears only where something will happen if pressed. Segment colours retire.

  2. Light first

    Design every screen for outdoor legibility, then derive the night palette. Body text at 4.5:1 minimum, large text 3:1.

  3. One thing per screen

    A menu handed to a confused person hands his confusion back. Ask one question, show one next step, offer one way out.

  4. The voice is a person, not an institution

    A serif carries the guide’s own sentences; a sans carries logistics. The split is meaningful and should be held consistently.

  5. Certainty over atmosphere

    Mood is welcome only after the question is answered. If a screen looks beautiful and leaves him unsure what to do, it has failed.

06 · Components

The inventory that does not exist yet

Twelve parts cover every screen in the product. Each needs all eight states drawn, not just the resting one — the missing states are where the current build hurts most.

  • 01Button — primary / secondary / ghost
  • 02Option — the large tap target
  • 03TextField — label, hint, error
  • 04Chip — single and multi select
  • 05Progress ticks
  • 06Panel — the raised surface
  • 07StopRow — collapsed and open
  • 08LostNote — the confusion callout
  • 09CostTag — money, always mono
  • 10LifelineBar — persistent, one tap
  • 11EmptyState
  • 12Error / toast
Eight states each: default, hover, focus-visible, active, selected, disabled, loading, error. There are currently zero focus styles in the entire codebase — the product cannot be operated by keyboard today.

07 · Scope

Screens to design, in order

  1. P0
    The Door

    One screen. The problem named, and one way out. No nav, no map, no menu.

  2. P0
    The Asking — four states

    Three questions, one per screen, then the handoff. Options are feelings, not categories.

  3. P1
    The Stop — collapsed and open

    The atom of the whole product. Carries what, why, how, cost, one tip, and the confusion note. Design this before the pages that contain it.

  4. P1
    The Plan

    Three movements: Settle, Your days, Keep. A skimmable spine by default, depth on tap.

  5. P2
    The Ground

    Stay, money, transport, SIM, water, safety, etiquette. Unglamorous and non-negotiable.

  6. P2
    The Walk

    Field register, in full. Now, next, lost. The register switch happens here.

08 · Out of scope

What we are removing

Each of these serves a real person — a browser, a gift-giver, a diaspora reader. None of them is Aklavya. Do not design around them.

  • The India map and twelve-city chapterHe is going to one city
  • Delhi, Jaipur, Kolkata, MumbaiVaranasi’s own content is unfinished
  • Segment pages as destinationsHe does not know those words
  • Cart, gift box, build-your-ownServes a gift-giver
  • NewsletterHe wants a trip, not a subscription
  • The sidebarA menu is his confusion, returned

09 · Acceptance

How to know a screen is finished

Run all seven before calling anything done. Any failure sends the screen back, however good it looks.

Squint

Blur your eyes. Is exactly one thing obviously the next action?

Sun

Legible on a phone at 30% brightness outdoors. Body 4.5:1, large text 3:1.

Panic

Can he reach a human in one tap, from this screen, without opening a menu?

Stranger

Does it still work for someone who reads no Devanagari and knows nothing of the city?

Vocabulary

Does it require knowing “Galiyan”, “Swaad” or “Hriday”? If yes, rewrite it.

Thumb

Every target 44px or larger, reachable one-handed on a 6-inch phone.

Keyboard

Tab through it. Every interactive element shows a visible focus ring.

The test behind all the others: does this make one anxious first-timer feel that someone is walking beside him? If answering takes a paragraph, the answer is no.
